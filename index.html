Quality of life is how New Yorkers judge whether the city works for them: a job that covers the rent, a school that teaches, a street that feels safe, a government that delivers. This edition walks through the brief’s evidence, then places its 50 ideas one at a time on a single New York block.

Quality of life isn’t a right-wing cause.

Mayor Zohran Mamdani has observed that “quality of life” and “efficiency” came to sound like conservative words, and he warned his own side against ceding the basics of city government because of it.

“If politics cannot deliver on the block where you live…”

Mayor Zohran Mamdani1

The brief takes him at his word. New Yorkers of every political stripe want work, a home they can afford, safe streets, good schools, and services worth their taxes. That should be common ground.

“Quality of life” is broader than its politics.

The phrase entered the policy vocabulary in the 1960s and ’70s to name the parts of human welfare that income alone misses: health, housing, education, the environment, the feel of daily life.

In New York it took on a narrower meaning in the Giuliani years, standing for orderly streets, clean public spaces, and a city government that sweats the small, cumulative stuff.

The brief uses the broad, practical sense.

Quality of life is the set of conditions that decide whether ordinary New Yorkers can build a good life here:

  1. 1Work that pays enough to afford a home and raise a family.
  2. 2A home within reach.
  3. 3Schools that prepare children well.
  4. 4Streets, parks, and transit that feel safe and orderly.
  5. 5A city government that delivers competent services at a fair cost.

Measured that way, the promise is under strain.

Jobs that let service and blue-collar workers get ahead have shrunk. Rising incomes have bought most people little. Housing and childcare cost a fortune. Schools and public safety add to the pressure.

The question underneath: can someone of ordinary means still come to New York and make a better life? The red marks on the block are the problems the brief documents.

First the evidence, then the 50 ideas.

Part 02 lays out the numbers behind the brief’s worry. Parts 03 through 09 take the recommendations in order. Each one lands on this block as a numbered pin, and the block changes as you scroll: blue marks what an idea adds, red marks the problem it targets.

The ideas draw on Manhattan Institute’s catalog of New York City research. Some are City Hall’s to do alone; others need Albany or another agency.

The middle rungs of the job ladder broke first.

Between 2019 and 2023, industries paying middle wages shed more than 76,000 jobs, a 4.7% drop, with construction hit hardest. Lower-wage industries grew only 1.5%.2

Those are the jobs that let people without a professional degree get established and support a family.

The weakness outlasted the rebound.

Six industries were still below their late-2019 employment at the end of 2025, according to the state comptroller.4 Youth unemployment averaged 11.9% in 2025.

A booming finance or tech sector can’t, by itself, give every New Yorker a route to a good living. Rebuilding the middle class takes a broader base of work and enterprise.5

Gains at the top, a flat line below.

From 2019 to 2024, real average income per rose 8.9%. For the bottom 90%, it fell 0.8%; adjusting for the growing share of single filers turns that into a 0.4% gain.6

Not everyone stood still: the New School found real wage gains in the middle of the pay scale.7 But the city needs more openings for people trying to find work or move up.

Housing ate what raises there were.

The city’s hit $3,707 a month in the spring of 2026, more than 30% above 2019: roughly $10,400 more a year.8

A city that needs electricians and line cooks has to let enough housing get built for them to live near their jobs.

Childcare costs as much as a second rent.

Infant and toddler care averaged $18,200 a year in family-based settings and $26,000 in centers in 2024.9 Between rent and care, a respectable salary can vanish.

Costs like these shape whether people have children, start businesses, or stay in New York at all.

Many children simply aren’t in class.

In 2023–24, 34.8% of city public-school students were . Among students in temporary housing, 51.7% were.10

Schools can’t do much for a child who routinely isn’t there. Tap the buttons on the chart to compare.

Fewer murders, but the streets aren’t fixed.

Through September 20, preliminary NYPD figures showed murders down 24.4% from the same stretch of 2025, and the down 6.34%.11

Yet felony assaults sit about 44% above 2019, and subway-system homicides run several times their old average.1213 Disorder and harassment still keep people out of public spaces.

The families walking out are the middle class.

Among families with children under five who left the city in 2023, 51% were middle income, compared with 43% of such families overall.14

People move for many reasons, so no single policy can be blamed. But City Hall should ask what would make staying more attractive. The answer matters just as much to those who can’t leave.

No accident

The brief’s diagnosis is that these conditions are mostly the product of choices.

Rents are high because decades of zoning and property-tax rules made rental housing hard and costly to build. Disorder lingers where enforcement has slackened. Children miss school when nobody is held responsible for getting them there. Across administrations, City Hall and Albany have left many New Yorkers paying more in taxes for weaker services. The pandemic and national trends, the authors argue, can’t explain why these problems have lasted.

The mayor invited fellow progressives to take quality of life seriously. The authors write that they want to help, that agreeing on the goal won’t settle every policy fight, and that if the mayor adopts these ideas and makes progress, they will welcome it.
